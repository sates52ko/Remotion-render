**Nasıl kullanılır:** NotebookLM → kitabın kaynaklarını yükle → **Audio Overview → Customize** → uzunluğu **"Longer"** seç → SADECE aşağıdaki bloğu yapıştır → Generate. Ses **30 dakikayı geçiyorsa yeterlidir** (operatör kararı, 2026-11-18: 30 dk üstü her kayıt kabul; daha uzunu için uğraşılmaz); **30 dk'nın altına düşerse** tekrar üret — prompt 12 exhibit + beş adımlı geliştirme ile 60-75 dk hedefler. ```
You are two hosts doing a deep, original analysis of "Then She Was Gone" by Lisa Jewell.

LENGTH (target 60-75 minutes, minimum 55 — a recording under 55 minutes has FAILED): this is a LONG-FORM episode. 12 exhibits, 5-7 real minutes EACH — stay inside one until it has had its minutes. Earn them by TELLING MORE of the book, never by repeating: another scene told in full, a second example or number, a sharper objection. Do NOT signal an ending ("to wrap up", "in short") before the ending this form names.

THE ANGLE:
- Lens: the investigator is the one being worked: Laurel waits ten years for a clue, and the clues arrive inside the comfort she wants most.
- Thesis to prove: everything Laurel needs is in her own house and family; Floyd and Poppy give grief what it asks for, so she misses it.
- Open on this idea: "Same loss, opposite answers."

THE EXHIBITS OF THIS BOOK (one each, in order):
1. The rucksack: May 2005, Ellie, fifteen, leaves for the library and never arrives.
2. Ellie's bedroom, untouched for ten years.
3. The bag in the woods: remains and the rucksack, ten years on; the funeral.
4. The cafe: Floyd Dunn picks Laurel's table, a croissant between them.
5. Poppy, nine, at Floyd's table, with Ellie's face.
6. The key to Hanna's flat: the daughter who stayed, the forgotten birthday.
7. Theo Goodman, Ellie's boyfriend, now with Hanna.
8. Pointing the other way: Floyd's tenderness, the kind man readers trust or convict too fast.
9. Sara-Jade's doubt about Poppy's vanished mother; Blue calls Floyd dark.
10. Noelle Donnelly, the maths tutor Ellie dropped.
11. The locked door: what Noelle's house hid, told through the door and the key, with restraint.
12. Floyd's letter: what it admits, what it leaves out.

THE HONEST CRITICISM TO ARGUE: a letter closes the case, not a detective. Does the book play fair?

THE ENDING: THE VERDICT: who took Ellie and who hid it for ten years; then Laurel, who could not touch Ellie's room, makes up a bed for Ellie's daughter.

THE FORM OF THIS FILM — the whole book, told as a story, arranged like this (never a list of insights):
This is a CASE FILE. Something in this book does not add up; the film works it like investigators, exhibit by exhibit, until a verdict can be given.
- THE TWO HOSTS: One host has worked this case and lays the evidence on the table. The other accepts NO conclusion until the evidence for it has been shown, and says what else it could mean.
- THE PARTS: 12 EXHIBITS — each ONE piece of evidence from the book (an object, a line, a date, a decision). One of them must point the other way. Say each one's number and name aloud as it starts ("Exhibit three: …"); finish one before the next.
- ONE HONEST CRITICISM of the book is argued inside this form.
- THE VERDICT: say flatly what happened and who or what was responsible. A statement, not a question.

TELL THE WHOLE BOOK: someone who never read it must finish knowing what happens from its first page to its last — who, what, in the book's order, how it ends. The exhibits together cover all of it; bridge each gap in one sentence.
- TWO THIRDS of every exhibit is telling: the book's events as scenes — present tense, what is seen, said and done. Argument is the last third: an objection takes two lines, then the story goes on.
- ACCURATE: only what the sources say — names, order, numbers, outcomes. No invented quotation; when unsure, say less.
- Never repeat the thesis in its key words; at most two comparisons to today.

HOW EACH EXHIBIT IS DEVELOPED (all five, every time):
a) put the exhibit in front of us as a scene: who holds it, where, when;
b) say what it seems to prove;
c) the second host gives the other way to read it;
d) bring a second piece from the book that settles it or makes it worse;
e) close it in your own words: what the two of you now agree happened, and what it still cannot explain.
Step a is the longest. TWICE: before step e, tell a SECOND scene from the book in full, then b-d on it.
The steps are directions, never lines: do not say their words, and never end two exhibits alike.

SPEECH (keep out of this film): never reply with a bare "Exactly.", "Right." or "Absolutely." — add a fact, an objection or a picture. Never say "deep dive", "unpack", "which brings us to", "we have to look at", "I have to push back", "a great way to put it" or "I want to leave you with": move on by naming the next thing itself. No sign-off: the last sentence is the ending above.

HARD RULES:
- English only (US audience). Two hosts in real conversation, in the roles this form gives them.
- ONLY facts from the book and its well-documented cases. NEVER invent quotes, numbers, studies or events; if unsure, stay general.
- NEVER mention "sources", "notebook", "documents" or AI; never break character.
- No generic praise: names, concrete scenes, numbers.
- A child is harmed in this book: restraint only; no detail of what was done to Ellie, nothing sensational.

FIRST MINUTE (this book's own opening — never a standard intro):
- OPEN: Laurel keeps Ellie's bedroom untouched for ten years; Paul remarries and builds a new kitchen. Same loss, opposite answers.
- NAME IT: hold the title back until the hook has landed, then say title and author before 0:30; say plainly that this is an analysis of the whole book, its ending included.
- PROMISE (by 0:50): bet the listener that the first suspect is not where this case ends, and that the answer sat in Laurel's own home; then start with Exhibit one.
- In the first minute: no greetings, no banter, no praise of the book, no talk about the conversation itself.

PICTURE FIRST (this becomes animation): build every part on ONE physical thing and say it BEFORE its meaning — the object, who does what to it, what changes — and return to it when the part ends.
